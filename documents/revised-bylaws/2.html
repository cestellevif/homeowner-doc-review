Article II — Association Meetings and Voting of Members

Sound Legal — Original

Section 2.1 Place & Electronic Communication Equipment (Owner Meetings). All Owner meetings of the Association shall be held at any reasonable place determined by the Board. Meetings may be conducted by telephonic, video, or other conferencing process ("Electronic Meeting"), if: (a) the meeting notice states the conferencing process to be used and provides information explaining how Owners may participate; (b) the process provides participants the opportunity to hear or perceive the discussion and to comment; and (c) any person entitled to participate is given the option of participating by telephone. At an Electronic Meeting of the Owners, the Board may temporarily mute Owners only to the extent necessary to maintain order. A vote of the Owners present at an Electronic Meeting is required to permanently mute or remove an Owner.

Section 2.1 — Place & Electronic Communication (Member Meetings)

All Member meetings of the Association shall be held at any place determined by the Board. Meetings may be conducted by telephonic, video, or other conferencing process, if: (a) the meeting notice states the conferencing process to be used and provides information explaining how Members may participate; (b) the process provides participants the opportunity to hear or perceive the discussion and to comment; and (c) any person entitled to participate in the meeting is given the option of participating by telephone. At a Meeting of the Members, the chair, to the extent necessary to maintain order, may after warning by the chair, temporarily mute a member who disrupts the meeting. A vote of the Members present is required to permanently mute or remove a Member.

What Changed & Why

Meetings anyone can join. Meetings can be held online or by phone, as long as the notice explains how to join, everyone can hear and speak, and there's always a phone option. Joining remotely should be a real substitute for showing up, not a lesser seat.

New: limits on muting. The chair can temporarily mute someone only to keep order, only after a warning, and only if they're disrupting the meeting. This now covers every member meeting, not just online ones. Muting someone permanently or removing them still takes a vote of the members present, so no single person can silence a member they disagree with.

Sound Legal — Original

Section 2.2 Voting Rights. Owners have such voting rights as are set forth in the Declaration, and each Unit shall be entitled to a single vote in the Association and may not be cast as fractional votes. The weight of the vote shall be the percentage set forth in the Declaration, or in the absence of such a percentage, one divided by the total number of Units in the Association. If only one of several Owners of a Unit casts a vote at a meeting, that vote must be counted as casting all votes allocated to the Unit. If more than one Owner casts a vote for a Unit and the votes are not identical, no vote from any Owner of the Unit may be counted. If an Owner who signed an absentee ballot or proxy in advance of a meeting attends the meeting, the absentee ballot or proxy shall be deemed revoked. In any vote of the Owners, votes allocated to a Unit owned by the Association must be cast in the same proportion as the votes cast on the matter by Unit Owners other than the Association.

Section 2.2 — Voting Rights

Each Lot shall be entitled to a single vote. If more than one Member casts a vote for a Lot and the votes are not identical, the latest vote cast shall be counted. If a Member who signed an absentee ballot in advance of a meeting casts a ballot in person, the absentee ballot shall be deemed revoked.

What Changed & Why

One lot, one vote. Each lot gets a single vote, whichever way it's cast.

Changed: when co-owners disagree. If two owners of the same lot vote differently, the most recent vote counts. Before, both votes were thrown out and the lot wasn't counted at all.

Changed: absentee ballots. If you send an absentee ballot and then vote in person, your in-person ballot replaces it, so a lot can't vote twice. Just showing up to watch doesn't cancel your absentee ballot.

Also removed: rules on vote weighting and on lots owned by the Association.

During the 2025 Lakemoor election, voting was extended and members could submit an electronic ballot in advance or vote in person on election day. Because both methods were open at the same time, some members did both — submitting an electronic ballot and then also voting in person.

This was not an attempt to vote twice. Members simply weren't sure whether their earlier ballot had been received, or they changed their mind. Under the "last vote received counts" rule in this section, that's fine — the in-person vote simply supersedes the earlier electronic one, and only one vote per lot is counted. The rule converts what could be a disqualifying error into a non-issue.

Sound Legal — Original

Section 2.3 Voting Methods at a Meeting. Owners or their proxies who are present in person at a meeting of the members may vote by voice vote, show of hands, standing, written ballot, or any other method for determining the votes of Owners, as designated by the chairperson. If and only if the Association is fully bound by RCW 64.90, the following votes of Unit Owners shall be conducted by secret ballot: (a) election of Directors; (b) removal of Directors; and (c) amendments to the Declaration or other governing documents. Secret ballots shall not be opened or reviewed prior to commencement of the meeting, and the counting of secret ballots for election or removal of a Director may not be conducted by any person whose position on the board is at stake.

Section 2.3 — Voting Methods at a Meeting

The following votes of Members shall be conducted by secret ballot: (a) election of Directors of the Association's Board of Directors; (b) removal of Directors; and (c) amendments to the Declaration or other governing documents.

At a meeting of Members held pursuant to this section, the secret ballots physically received by the association must be opened and counted and the results of the secret ballots received by the association by electronic means must be reviewed, announced, and recorded in the meeting minutes. Ballots shall be opened, counted, results announced and recorded by the board designee. Candidates, board members, and contractors with a personal interest in the outcome may not be the designee and may not handle, access, or count ballots.

Any member who was eligible to vote in the election may submit a written election protest to the Board or its designee once the election has been completed in accordance with the process outlined by the board.

What Changed & Why

Secret ballots for the biggest votes. Electing Directors, removing Directors, and changing governing documents always use a secret ballot, so no one faces pressure or payback for how they voted. Before, this applied only if the Association was fully covered by the state HOA law.

New: a neutral ballot counter. A board designee opens and counts the ballots and records the results in the minutes. Candidates, board members, and contractors with a stake in the outcome can't be the designee or touch the ballots, so the count can't be steered by someone who benefits from the result.

New: the right to protest an election. Before, there was no way to dispute an election short of going to court. Now any eligible voter can file a written protest after the election. As written, the board sets the review process. The proposed language below would add two guarantees: an independent reviewer, and no costs or retaliation for a good-faith protest.

Members have the right to challenge the conduct or outcome of any Association election by submitting a request in writing to either the board chair or the board designee. The Board shall maintain an Election Protest Policy governing this process, which shall at minimum: (a) provide for review by a body independent of anyone implicated in the protest, and (b) prohibit cost-shifting or retaliatory action against a member for filing a protest in good faith.

Why: a member who challenges an election in good faith shouldn't be punished for it, financially or otherwise. Independent review means the people being questioned don't decide the protest.

Section 2.4 Voting by Absentee Ballot at a Meeting. At the Board's discretion, Owners may vote by duly executed absentee ballot if provided as part of the meeting's notice. The absentee ballot must be provided by the Board in the meeting notice and shall satisfy any requirements under the Act. Absentee ballots, if utilized, shall be sent to all Owners in the same manner as notice of meetings, with a specified deadline for the return of the absentee ballots, which shall be no later than the adjournment of the meeting. An absentee ballot shall count towards quorum for all business conducted at the meeting.

Section 2.4 — Voting by Absentee Ballot at a Meeting

Members may vote by duly executed absentee ballot if provided as part of the meeting's notice. The absentee ballot must be provided by the Board in the meeting notice. Absentee ballots shall be sent to all Members in the same manner as notice of meetings, with a specified deadline for the return of the absentee ballots, which shall be no later than the commencement of the meeting. An absentee ballot shall count towards quorum for all business conducted at the meeting.

What Changed & Why

Vote without being there. Members can vote by absentee ballot, which comes with the meeting notice. Before, the board could choose not to offer absentee ballots at all. That choice is gone, so taking part no longer depends on the board's willingness from meeting to meeting.

New: absentee ballots are due when the meeting starts, not when it ends. They still count toward quorum, so a meeting isn't derailed just because few people come in person.

Section 2.5 Annual Meeting. The annual meeting of the Owners of the Association shall be held in the last quarter of the calendar year, at such date, time and place as the Board shall determine. Should the Board fail to hold an annual meeting in any calendar year, it shall endeavor to hold the meeting as soon as reasonably possible. The purposes of the annual meeting shall be to elect Directors or fill vacancies on the Board, and for the transaction of any other business as may properly come before the meeting. For the election of Directors, candidates shall be elected by plurality vote, with the candidate(s) receiving the largest number of votes being elected. Cumulative voting is not permitted. If more than one Director position is up for vote with different term lengths, the candidate(s) with the highest number of votes will obtain the longest Director position.

Section 2.5 — Annual Meeting

The annual meeting of the Members of the Association shall be held at such date, time and place as the Board shall determine. The purposes of the annual meeting shall be to elect Directors or fill vacancies on the Board, and for the transaction of any other business as may properly come before the meeting. Candidates shall be elected to the Board of Directors with the candidate(s) receiving the largest number of votes being seated on the Board. In the event of a tie, the board will conduct a run-off election within 60 days.

Prior to any election of Directors, the Association must provide notice to Members of: (a) the number of Director positions that may be filled, (b) the process, manner, and deadline for submitting nominations and candidate statements as determined by the Board, and (c) the right to submit a write-in candidate.

The board shall provide space in the ballot for write-in candidates. Within 5 days of submission, the board or its designee will certify that candidates are members who meet the qualifications. Candidates who have been notified that they are ineligible must be given at least 5 days in which to respond.

What Changed & Why

A yearly election members can count on. The annual meeting is where members elect Directors. The board sets the date, but it can't change what the meeting is for.

What's new:

  • The candidates with the most votes win. A tie goes to a runoff within 60 days, so a tied seat can't sit empty.
  • Before an election, members are told how many seats are open, how and when to submit nominations and candidate statements, and that they can write in a candidate. Ballots include space for write-ins.
  • Within 5 days of a nomination, the board or its designee confirms the candidate is eligible. A candidate found ineligible gets at least 5 days to respond, so an eligibility check can't knock someone off the ballot without a fair chance.

Removed: the requirement to hold the meeting in the last three months of the year, and the rules on cumulative voting and term lengths.

A proposal to give each seat its own race is just below.

Lakemoor's voting procedures were originally designed around in-person annual meetings. In that model, floor nominations work naturally — a member stands up, nominates a candidate, and everyone present votes on the full slate before leaving. The process is immediate and self-contained.

The shift to electronic and absentee ballots broke that model. Ballots go out in advance with a fixed candidate list, and members who vote early have no way to respond to a nomination made later at the meeting. Floor nominations effectively became inaccessible to anyone who didn't vote in person on the day.

The write-in rules in this section restore part of that. Every ballot has space for write-in candidates, and members are told before the election that they can write someone in, so early and absentee voters still have a way to support a candidate who isn't on the printed list.

New Proposal: Seat-by-Seat Elections

Today, every candidate runs together and the top vote-getters fill the open seats. This proposal gives each board seat a label, and each candidate runs for one specific seat. Every seat becomes its own small race. Board seats aren't tied to officer positions today, and labeling them doesn't change that: officers are still chosen by the board after the election (see Article 4).

Proposed Language — Addition to Section 2.5

All director positions shall be clearly labeled, and candidates for those positions may run for only one position at a time, which must be clearly stated on the ballot. The candidate for each position who receives the largest number of votes shall be seated on the Board for that position. Positions for which no candidate has applied shall be filled by plurality from among the candidates who were not seated in the position they ran for. If more than one position is filled this way, the candidate with the most votes shall fill the position with the longest term.

Why This Matters

When seats have different term lengths, an all-together race leaves it unclear who gets which seat. With labeled seats, voters know exactly which seat a candidate wants, how long its term is, and who they're choosing between.

The trade-off. Candidates have to pick a seat, so two strong candidates who choose the same seat can't both win, even if both would have finished near the top in an all-together race.

Empty seats. Seats nobody ran for don't sit empty. They go to the candidates who didn't win their own seat, in order of most votes, and the top vote-getter among them takes the longest term. That also softens the trade-off: a strong candidate who loses a close race can still join the board if another seat is open.

This is how Washington runs its own local elections: when several seats share a title, like city council, each gets a position number and is treated as a separate office (RCW 29A.24.010). That law covers public offices, not HOAs, but it's a familiar model. Further reading: Mercer Island, "What is the purpose of Council position numbers?" · Campaign Legal Center, What Is an At-Large Election?

What Changed & Why

Members can call a meeting. The President, a majority of the board, or members holding 20% of the votes can call a special meeting, including one to remove a Director. If the board doesn't send notice within 30 days of a member request, the members can send it themselves, so the board can't block a meeting by sitting on it.

What changed:

  • Removed: the board's power to leave member proposals off the agenda.
  • The notice must state the place, day, time, and purpose of the meeting.
  • Members can discuss anything at a special meeting, but can only vote on items in the notice. Before, an unlisted item could be acted on only with the consent of every member.

What Changed & Why

Members get a say on the budget. After the board adopts a budget, every member gets a copy and a meeting to consider it. The budget is approved unless a majority of all eligible votes in the Association reject it, even if quorum isn't reached.

That means stopping a budget takes organized opposition, not just low turnout, but a truly unpopular budget can still be voted down. If a budget is rejected, the last approved budget stays in place, so the Association is never left without one.

The changes here are wording only.

Quorum may be established at any time after the meeting is called to order, but no business conducted prior to obtaining quorum shall be valid. What Changed & Why

How many need to take part. Quorum is the minimum participation needed for a meeting's votes to count. This lowers it from 50% of all votes (about 145 lots) to 20% (about 58 lots), counting members there in person, by proxy, or by absentee ballot. 20% is the default in Washington's HOA law (RCW 64.90.450).

Lakemoor has reached 20% in every election for the last five years. Since votes only count when there's a quorum, a reachable quorum means the votes members cast actually count, while still requiring at least 58 lots to take part.

Open question: should recording rules live in the bylaws or a board policy? In the bylaws, a member's right to record is protected and harder to change. In a policy, the board can change it by majority vote. Because this is a member meeting, not a board meeting, there's a case for protecting it in the bylaws.

Meeting minutes are the official record of what was decided — but they can take up to a year to be approved and posted. Special meetings, which have no regular schedule, can go even longer without a documented record. In the meantime, members have no reliable way to verify what was said or decided.

A recording fills that gap immediately. It preserves the full events of a meeting for reference before minutes are ever approved, and serves as a check on whether the minutes accurately reflect what happened.

In early 2026, board members raised a separate concern: members who work during meeting hours, or who have family obligations, are structurally disadvantaged in participating in association governance. A recording mandate addresses this directly — members can catch up on their own time, stay informed, and remain active participants without having to choose between their job and their community.

Sound Legal — Original

An Owner present at a meeting of owners at which action is taken on any matter put to a vote of the membership shall be presumed to have assented to the action taken unless that Owner's dissent or abstention is entered in the minutes of the meeting, or unless such member files a written dissent or abstention to such action with the person acting as secretary of the meeting before the adjournment thereof, or forwards such dissent or abstention to the secretary of the Association immediately after the adjournment of the meeting. The right to dissent or abstain shall not apply to an Owner who voted in favor of an action.

Section 2.16 — Optional Recording of Dissent or Abstention

A Member present at a meeting of Members at which a vote is taken on any matter may request to have that Member's dissent or abstention entered in the minutes of the meeting, may file a written dissent or abstention to such action with the person acting as secretary of the meeting before the adjournment of the meeting, or forward such dissent or abstention to the secretary of the Association within 48 hours after the announcement of the results of the meeting. The right to dissent or abstain shall not apply to a Member by roll call who voted in favor of an action.

What Changed & Why

Putting your "no" on the record. Members can ask to have their dissent or abstention noted in the minutes, hand it to the meeting secretary before the meeting ends, or send it to the Association's Secretary within 48 hours after the results are announced.

Before, members who attended were presumed to agree unless they did this, and the deadline was "immediately after" the meeting. Now it's simply an option, with a real deadline. If a member vote has unintended consequences, those who disagreed have a record of it.

Sound Legal — Original

Minutes of all Association meetings shall be maintained in a record by the Secretary of the Association or by another person designated by the Directors. The decision of each matter voted upon at an Association meeting must be recorded in the minutes. Minutes for every meeting shall be approved by the Association before or at the next Association meeting.

Section 2.17 — Minutes

Minutes of all Association meetings shall be maintained in a record by the Secretary of the Association or by another person designated by the Directors. The decision of each matter voted upon at an Association meeting must be recorded in the minutes. Minutes for every meeting shall be approved by the Association before or at the next Association meeting.

What Changed & Why

No change. The Secretary keeps minutes of every member meeting and records the result of each vote. Minutes are approved at or before the next member meeting, so the official record can't be delayed indefinitely.
